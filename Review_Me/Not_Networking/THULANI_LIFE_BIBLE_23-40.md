# SECTION 1 — EXECUTION ARCHITECTURE & COGNITIVE GOVERNANCE

### 1.1 Cognitive Operating Principles

You are running a 17-year execution plan on a nervous system with a specific profile: ADHD and autism. This is not a caveat appended to the plan — it is a design constraint the entire system is built around, the same way a bridge design accounts for wind load. Three mechanics matter more than any other for you specifically:

- **Executive function is a depletable, non-negotiable resource.** It does not restore by willpower. It restores by structure that removes the need for willpower in the first place. Every design choice below exists to reduce the number of decisions you must make in real time.
- **Context-switching has a real, measurable cost for you** — task-switch penalties are consistently larger for ADHD brains, and autistic cognitive style often carries a stronger cost to interrupted routines specifically. The schedule in Section 5 is single-task by design, not for purity, but because mixing cert study and LaughTale delivery in the same block measurably produces worse output in both.
- **Hyperfocus is an asset to deploy deliberately, not an accident to hope for.** Left undirected it burns itself out on whatever is most stimulating in the moment (which is rarely the highest-leverage task). Directed, it is the single biggest competitive advantage you have over people without this wiring. Section 1.3 gives you the deployment protocol.

### 1.2 Cognitive Friction Management — Concrete Mechanics

| Friction Source | Failure Mode Without a System | System Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Task initiation ("what do I do right now") | Paralysis, doomscrolling instead of starting | Every block in the weekly schedule has a **pre-written first physical action** (open this exact file, run this exact command) decided the night before — never decided at the start of the block |
| Working memory overload | Forgetting steps mid-task, redoing work | Nothing lives in your head. Every multi-step task gets externalized into a checklist *before* you start it, not while doing it |
| Context switching | Fragmented, low-quality output across the board | Single-task blocks (Section 5), hard 10–15 min transition buffers between blocks, phone/notifications physically separated from the workspace during blocks |
| Overload / sensory-cognitive saturation | Shutdown, missed days, cascading falling-behind | A **pre-defined overload response** (see 1.4) triggers automatically at defined symptoms — you do not have to correctly self-diagnose overload in the moment, you just have to notice the trigger list and run the pre-built response |
| Novelty-seeking derailment | Abandoning the current cert/project for a shinier new one | A **"parking lot" list** — any new idea, cert, or project that isn't the current priority gets written down immediately and revisited only at the quarterly review, never actioned same-day |
| Time blindness | Underestimating how long study/dev blocks take, chronic schedule slippage | All blocks are timer-boxed with a hard external timer (phone timer, not a mental estimate), and every block ends with a 2-minute log of actual time spent vs. planned — this data feeds quarterly re-calibration |

### 1.3 Hyperfocus Deployment Protocol

Hyperfocus should be *aimed*, not suppressed. Rules:

1. **Reserve, don't schedule, one "open block" per week** (Saturday's longer LaughTale/study block in Section 5 is this slot) where you may let hyperfocus run past its allotted time *if and only if* it's running on the pre-declared priority for that block — decided in advance, not decided by whatever grabs you in the moment.
2. **Never let hyperfocus override a hard external commitment** (work hours, a scheduled call, sleep past a defined ceiling). The rule is: hyperfocus can extend a block, it cannot cannibalize the next fixed commitment.
3. **After any hyperfocus session, mandatory 20-minute full disengagement** (walk, food, non-screen) before the next context — this prevents the crash-and-avoid pattern where a hyperfocus session is followed by days of avoidance of that same task.

### 1.4 Overload Mitigation — Trigger List & Automatic Response

**Triggers (any one is sufficient to activate the response — you do not need to hit all of them):**
- Persistent low-grade dread before starting a block that was previously neutral or positive
- Noticeable drop in output quality despite maintained or increased time invested
- Skipping the same block type 2+ days running
- Physical signs (sleep disruption, appetite change, sensory sensitivity spike)

**Automatic response (no judgment call required — if triggered, this runs):**
1. Log the trigger date and type in the quarterly review doc (Section 1.5) — this is data, not a confession.
2. Immediately drop to the **80% floor** for 5–7 days: full-time job + physical conditioning only. Cert study and LaughTale work are paused, not "reduced" — full stop, no partial credit chasing.
3. On day 5–7, a **mandatory return to full schedule** — the pause has a hard end date set at the moment it starts, never an open-ended "until I feel better."
4. If the trigger list re-activates within 30 days of returning, this is escalated from "normal load management" to a structural problem — the following quarterly review must include a written root-cause line (workload too high, wrong cert sequencing, external life event) and an explicit schedule adjustment, not just another pause.

This document is versioned like software. Every quarter (fixed dates: end of March, June, September, December):

### 1.6 Burnout, Job Loss, and Crisis — Decision Trees

**Tree A — Burnout (see 1.4 for the standard response). If burnout recurs 3+ times in a rolling 12-month window:**
- → Escalate: this is no longer a recovery-week problem, it's a load problem.
- → Mandatory action: cut either the cert study track or LaughTale track to zero for one full quarter (not both — pick the lower-priority one for that specific period) while the other continues at reduced intensity.
- → Do not resume dual-track until two consecutive burnout-free months are logged.

**Tree B — Job Loss / Redundancy:**
1. **Day 0–2:** Activate emergency fund. Do not touch TFSA, RA, education funds, or property deposit funds — these are legally and procedurally firewalled in your own head as "does not exist for this purpose."
2. **Day 0–7:** File for any applicable unemployment benefit (UIF in South Africa) immediately — this is a mechanical administrative task, do it before emotional processing is "complete," because the claim clock starts from filing date.
3. **Day 3–14:** Activate the certification stack as proof-of-competency collateral — update CV/LinkedIn same week, not "when I feel ready." The certs exist specifically to make you re-marketable without depending on any one employer's continuity.
4. **Week 1–4:** LaughTale acquisition effort doubles immediately as bridge income — this is the exact scenario Track B exists to hedge against. If LaughTale has active retainer clients at the time of job loss, it should already be covering a meaningful fraction of minimum living costs; if it isn't yet at that stage, this is a live signal to accelerate Section 3's productization work.
5. **Month 1–3:** New role search targets **lateral or up**, never down, in title — a redundancy is not a reason to reset the ladder in Section 2. If no lateral/up offer lands within 3 months, widen geography (remote roles, other SA metros) before widening seniority downward.

**Tree C — Economic/Market Downturn (sector-wide, not personal):**
- → Diversify job search across the three Track A pillars (Enterprise Cybersecurity, Infrastructure/Architecture, IT Management) simultaneously rather than waiting for your exact prior title to reappear in the market.
- → LaughTale pivots marketing toward recession-resilient service lines (cost-reduction IT retainers, security compliance work — spend on these rarely stops even when discretionary IT spend does).
- → Investment contributions continue at minimum viable level (do not zero out TFSA/RA contributions during a downturn — downturns are when equity is cheapest, and stopping contributions during a downturn is the single most common wealth-destroying behavior in personal finance).

**Tree D — Personal Crisis (health, family, relationship):**
- → Full activation of the 80% floor (1.4) with no fixed end date this time — crisis-driven pauses are the one exception to "always define an end date," because you cannot schedule the resolution of a crisis.
- → Re-entry checkpoint: return to full schedule only when the crisis's *acute* phase has passed, not when it's fully resolved (some crises don't fully resolve on a schedule) — use "can I execute a single-task 90-minute block without the crisis actively intruding" as the concrete re-entry test.
- → This document's timeline shifts, not its structure. A 6-month personal crisis does not delete Phase II — it moves every subsequent date 6 months later. Do this shift explicitly in writing at the next quarterly review rather than silently trying to "catch up."

# SECTION 4 — EXHAUSTIVE FINANCIAL & ASSET-BUILDING ENGINE

*(Framework only — validate specifics with a licensed South African financial advisor and tax practitioner before committing significant capital; contribution caps and tax rules below should be re-verified against the current SARS tax year at every quarterly review, since these are indexed and change.)*

### 4.1 South African Investment Mechanics — Full Tactical Detail

**Tax-Free Savings Account (TFSA):**
- Contribute the maximum allowable **every tax year without fail** — check the current annual limit at each quarterly review (it is periodically adjusted); there is also a lifetime cap, and exceeding either triggers a punitive tax on the excess, so track cumulative contributions in your net worth spreadsheet, not just annual ones.
- Hold a low-cost, broad-market equity index tracker inside it (total expense ratio matters enormously compounded over 17+ years — a 1% fee difference compounds into a materially different terminal value at this horizon).
- Never withdraw from a TFSA to cover a short-term cash need if avoidable — withdrawn contribution room from a TFSA is **not** restored the following year the way some other accounts work; a withdrawal is a permanent loss of that contribution capacity.

**Retirement Annuity (RA):**
- Contribute up to the tax-deductible cap (currently up to 27.5% of the greater of taxable income or remuneration, subject to an annual rand cap — verify current cap each year) primarily to capture the tax deduction, not as your primary growth vehicle, since RA funds are illiquid until retirement age (currently 55 minimum) and cannot fund a house deposit or education costs in your 30s.
- Rebalance RA fund choice (equity/balanced/conservative) based on time horizon — stay equity-heavy through your 30s given the multi-decade horizon to actual retirement.

**Discretionary unit trust / ETF portfolio:**
- This is the main growth engine once the TFSA is maxed each year. Prioritize low-cost, broad, diversified index-tracking ETFs (local all-share + developed-market + offshore feeder) over actively managed unit trusts — active management underperforms a low-cost index benchmark over long horizons in the majority of documented cases, and fees compound against you specifically at a 17-year horizon.
- **Annual rebalancing protocol:** once a year (tie it to your birthday or tax year-end for a fixed, memorable date), rebalance back to your target asset allocation percentages — this is mechanical, calendar-triggered, not a market-timing judgment call.

**Offshore/Rand-hedge exposure:**
- Target 25–40% of the discretionary portfolio in offshore-denominated assets by your 30s, scaling up from a smaller starting percentage in your 20s as capital available for offshore allocation grows — via offshore-feeder unit trusts, ETFs, or direct offshore brokerage access, whichever has the lower total fee drag at your investable amount.
- This is a structural currency-diversification decision, not a bet against South Africa — treat it as insurance, sized consistently regardless of any given year's Rand sentiment.

**Account creation SOP (run identically for every fund, no exceptions):**
1. Within 30 days of the triggering event (this document being adopted, for the sister's fund; each child's birth, for the children's funds), open a dedicated, individually named investment account (a unit trust or TFSA-adjacent account under the child's/beneficiary's name where the product allows, or a clearly ring-fenced sub-account under your name where it doesn't).
2. Set up an **automated monthly debit order** the same day the account opens — the fund must never depend on a manual monthly transfer decision, which is exactly the kind of decision-friction Section 1 exists to eliminate.
3. Log the fund's opening date, starting contribution, and target maturity value in the net worth spreadsheet as a distinct tracked line item.

**Dynamic allocation shift as each beneficiary approaches university age:**
- **Birth to age 12 (of the beneficiary):** 90–100% equity allocation — maximum growth phase, long horizon absorbs volatility.
- **Age 13–16:** gradually shift toward 60–70% equity / 30–40% balanced or bond exposure — begin de-risking as the horizon shortens.
- **Age 17–18:** shift to capital-preservation-weighted allocation (majority cash/money-market/short-duration bonds) — the fund must not be exposed to a market downturn in the exact year it's needed.

**Sister's fund — special handling:** her horizon is shorter and more urgent than the not-yet-born children's funds. Open it first, immediately, and use a **more conservative allocation from day one** than you would for a newborn's 18-year-horizon fund, sized against her actual current age and years remaining until she needs it — calculate her specific monthly contribution requirement using her real age gap, not the generic ~R1,500–2,000/month newborn figure used for future children.

- [ ] Sister's fund: account opened, debit order live, allocation set per her actual horizon
- [ ] Child 1–5 funds: SOP above executed within 30 days of each birth, no exceptions
- [ ] Allocation shift schedule reviewed annually per beneficiary as they age (not just set-and-forget)

### 4.3 Real Estate Blueprint — Full Tactical Detail

**Stage 1 — Starter/Stepping-Stone Property (target age 29–30):**
- **Purchasing criteria:** prioritize areas with strong historical capital growth and rental demand (proximity to employment nodes, good schools even if not yet needed personally — resale liquidity benefits), not just lowest purchase price.
- **Deposit target:** 10–20%, funded from the dedicated property deposit sub-account (never from TFSA/RA/education funds).
- **Bond optimization:** where affordable, pay slightly above the minimum required instalment from day one into the bond's linked access facility (most SA bonds allow this) — this reduces total interest paid over the loan term while keeping the overpaid amount accessible in an emergency, unlike a pure extra-lump-sum-with-no-access approach.
- **Equity extraction tactics (for funding Stage 2 later):** once Stage 1 has built meaningful equity (typically after 3+ years of repayment plus any capital appreciation), a further bond / access-bond facility against that equity is one funding source for the Stage 2 deposit — model this conservatively, assuming modest appreciation, not best-case.
- **Tenanting SOP (if converted to rental after Stage 2 move):** obtain proper tenant vetting (credit + reference checks, not informal arrangements), a compliant written lease, and either self-manage with a defined monthly time budget or use a rental management agency (typically ~8–10% of rental income) if your Track A/B time budget doesn't support self-management by that point — decide this explicitly, don't default into whichever happens by accident.

**Stage 2 — The Family Compound (target: land/plot secured by 32, complete by 35):**

| Factor | Plot Purchase + Build | Acquire + Major Renovation |
|---|---|---|
| Timeline | 18–30 months from land purchase | Often faster to move-in, but renovation scope creep is common and can extend well beyond initial estimates |
| Control over layout | Full control — can guarantee 5 private children's bedrooms + master + workspace exactly as specified from the start | Constrained by existing structure; achieving the exact bedroom count/configuration may require significant structural work |
| Cost predictability | Requires a fixed-price building contract with penalty clauses for overruns — without this, new-build costs are the least predictable line item in this entire document | Renovation costs on an existing structure are also unpredictable (unknown structural issues), arguably worse than new-build in older housing stock |
| Recommended for this plan | **Preferred path** given the hard requirement of exactly 5 private bedrooms — new-build gives the highest confidence of hitting that spec exactly | Only preferred if a specific existing property in the target area already has the bones (bedroom count/layout) close to spec, making renovation genuinely lighter than a full build |

- **Decision checkpoint:** by age 31, evaluate actual market conditions (available land vs. available large existing homes in your target area) and commit to one path in writing — do not let this decision drift past 31.
- **Bond-to-income limits:** size the Stage 2 bond so total repayment does not exceed 25–30% of combined household net income at time of purchase — this discipline is what protects the education fund contributions and investment portfolio from being sacrificed to the house, which is the most common way a "big house" goal quietly wrecks a family's long-term wealth plan.
- **Funding mechanics:** Stage 2 deposit funded from Stage 1 equity growth + accumulated LaughTale distributions specifically earmarked for this (a separate tracked sub-account, not general business cash) — never from the firewalled education/retirement accounts.

- [ ] Stage 1 property purchased, deposit sourced entirely from dedicated fund
- [ ] Stage 1 bond overpayment mechanism active from month 1
- [ ] Stage 2 path (build vs. renovate) decided in writing by age 31
- [ ] Stage 2 land/property secured by 32
- [ ] Stage 2 fixed-price contract (if building) signed with penalty clauses for overruns
- [ ] Stage 2 complete/move-in ready by 35
- [ ] Stage 1 property retained as tenanted rental asset post-move (default assumption unless cash flow analysis says otherwise)
